How to choose cycling gloves

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

The best cycling glove for you depends on how you ride: choose full-finger for cooler weather and off-road protection, fingerless for warm-weather road riding. Focus on padding placement that matches your riding position, a secure closure, and materials that breathe. Avoid overpaying for features you don’t need, a $20 pair can be just as reliable as a $100 pair for casual riding.

Full finger or fingerless? Which cycling glove style is right for you?

Full-finger gloves cover each digit and are the go-to for mountain biking, gravel riding, and any cooler-weather session. They protect against scrapes from branches or crashes and add warmth when temperatures drop below about 60°F. That extra coverage can feel a bit stuffy on a hot summer climb, though. Fingerless gloves (often called “half-finger” or “mitts”) leave your fingertips exposed, giving you maximum dexterity for brake and shift levers. They’re the classic choice for road cycling and commuting in warm weather because they breathe better and let you feel the controls directly. The downside: your fingers are vulnerable to sunburn and minor abrasions if you go down. For most riders, a good strategy is to own one of each. If you can only buy one pair, think about the climate and terrain you ride most often. Fingerless gloves are more versatile for fair-weather roadies; full-finger gloves are a better investment if you ride trails or live in a cooler area.

How much padding do you really need?

Padding in cycling gloves is designed to reduce pressure on the ulnar nerve, the nerve that runs through your palm, which can cause numbness or tingling on longer rides. Road cyclists who spend hours in a dropped position often benefit from a medium-thick gel or foam pad across the heel of the hand. Mountain bikers, on the other hand, usually prefer a thinner pad that provides just enough vibration damping without interfering with grip and bar feel. The key is matching padding to your riding style and bike setup. If you ride a road bike with a low, aggressive position, your upper body weight presses through your palms, so more padding helps. On a flat-bar hybrid or upright cruiser, your weight is more evenly distributed, and heavy padding can actually create pressure points. For most commuters and casual weekend riders, a light or “minimal” pad is plenty. Be wary of gloves with huge, bulky pads. They can cause you to grip the bar tighter, the opposite of what you want. The best approach is to try on a few pairs and simulate your riding position: sit on a stationary bike or lean forward in the store to feel where the padding lands. You want it under the meaty part of your palm, not the center or the fingers.

Fit and closure: What makes a glove stay put?

A cycling glove should fit like a second skin, snug but not tight. There should be no excess fabric at the fingertips (for full-finger models) and no bunching across the palm. If you can pinch more than a quarter-inch of material at the back of the hand, the size is too big. A good test: when you make a fist, the glove shouldn’t pull your skin or restrict movement of your thumb and index finger. Closures range from simple elastic wrists to hook-and-loop (Velcro) straps. Elastic is fine for very casual gloves and keeps the profile clean, but it can lose tension over time. Hook-and-loop is more adjustable and secure, especially for full-finger gloves that you pull on and off often. Look for a strap that’s long enough to accommodate a range of wrist sizes but doesn’t overlap so much that it bunches under a long-sleeve jersey. Silicone grippers on the fingers or palm are a recent innovation that help you hold the bar with less effort. They are especially useful on full-finger gloves when riding in the rain or sweat. But they can wear down after a season, so check reviews for long-term durability.

Materials and breathability: What should you look for?

The main body of most cycling gloves is a blend of synthetic leather (like Clarino or Amara) on the palm and a breathable mesh or spandex on the back of the hand. The palm material needs to be abrasion resistant and grippy when wet. Look for “synthetic suede” or “microfiber” palms, they usually offer a good balance of durability and feel. Genuine leather palms are rare now and tend to be heavier and less breathable. Breathability comes from the back-of-hand material. Many gloves use a stretch mesh that vents heat well. For hot-weather riding, consider gloves with a terry cloth thumb panel to wipe sweat from your brow, a small but very useful feature. For cold-weather or winter commuting, a windproof or thermal-backed glove with a longer cuff is worth the investment; the same palm material works, but the back changes to fleece or softshell. Washability matters. Gloves get filthy, especially the palms. Most cycling gloves can be thrown in a gentle machine wash (cold water, hang dry), but check the label. Avoid gloves with stiff leather or non-removable padding that can degrade in the wash. A good rule: if the palm starts to feel stiff or shiny after a few rides, it’s time to wash.

Touchscreen compatibility: Worth the upgrade?

Many cycling gloves now include conductive threads in the thumb and index fingertips so you can operate a phone or GPS without taking the gloves off. This is genuinely handy for checking directions, taking a call, or adjusting a music playlist mid-ride. But the conductivity varies widely, sometimes it only works on certain devices or stops working after a few washes. If you rely on your phone for navigation or safety (calls, texts), touchscreen compatibility is a nice-to-have, but don’t base your whole purchase on it. Test the gloves with your phone before buying if possible. And remember: even the best conductive tip won’t be as responsive as your bare finger. For serious navigation, a handlebar mount is more reliable than poking at a phone with a gloved finger.

The main trade-off is durability, seams may fray or padding may flatten faster. But for occasional weekend rides or commuting, they are perfectly fine. Our advice: if you ride more than once a week, skip the absolute cheapest gloves and invest in something that will be comfortable for hours. If you ride very rarely, the budget pair will do.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to wear cycling gloves if I have padded cycling shorts?

Yes, gloves and shorts target different pressure points. Padded shorts reduce saddle discomfort, but your hands still need protection from road vibration and pressure on your palms. The two work together, not as replacements.

How often should I replace my cycling gloves?

Plan on replacing cycling gloves every 1 to 2 years with regular use, or sooner if the palm material feels slick, padding has flattened, or seams are unraveling. Gloves that no longer stay snug or cause numbness should be retired immediately.

Can I use weightlifting or work gloves for cycling?

You can, but they aren’t designed for the same grip or vibration damping. Cycling gloves have padding placed for bent-wrist positions, breathable backs, and often a sweat wipe. For a short, slow commute, work gloves might suffice, but they’ll likely be uncomfortable on a longer or faster ride.

What’s the best way to wash cycling gloves?

Most synthetic gloves are machine-washable on a gentle cycle with cold water. Use a mild detergent and air dry them away from direct heat. Never use fabric softener, it can degrade the grip and touchscreen conductivity. For quick freshening, hand-wash with mild soap and rinse thoroughly.

Are fingerless gloves safe for mountain biking?

Not typically recommended for serious mountain biking. Fingerless gloves leave your fingers exposed to branches, rocks, and crashes. Most mountain bikers wear full-finger gloves with reinforced knuckles for abrasion and impact protection. For light gravel or smooth singletrack, fingerless might be okay, but full finger is safer for trail riding.

Why do some cycling gloves have a tab on the middle finger?

That pull tab helps you take the glove off without turning it inside out. It’s a simple convenience feature that also protects the stitching and padding from being stressed during removal. It’s common on mid-range to premium gloves and a sign of added design thought.

In shortThe right cycling glove keeps your hands comfortable, protected, and in control for miles. Focus on fit, function, and the conditions you actually ride in, not marketing hype. Whether you go full-finger or fingerless, a pair that suits your specific riding style will make every pedal stroke more enjoyable and could prevent hand fatigue or injury over the long haul.
